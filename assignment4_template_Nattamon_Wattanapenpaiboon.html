Topic of this work

This blog post investigates whether the cultural idea of the Seven-Year Itch—the belief that marriages tend to break down around the seven-year mark—has any empirical support in global divorce data. Although the concept is widely referenced in popular culture, it has rarely been examined using large-scale longitudinal evidence. By analysing divorce patterns across countries and over time, this dataset provides a unique opportunity to evaluate a familiar cultural belief through a demographic lens.

Data description

This analysis uses the Divorces by duration of marriage dataset from the United Nations Statistics Division (UNSD), which provides official administrative counts of all recorded divorces by duration of marriage. These are administrative census data derived from national civil registration systems, making them well‑suited to examining when divorces typically occur and whether they cluster around the so‑called “Seven‑Year Itch”. The dataset covers 105 countries, with the most consistent reporting for European countries. For this analysis, the period 1995-2025 is used to provide a clean and comparable 30-year window. It is released under the UNdata Terms of Use, permitting academic reuse with attribution, and contains only aggregated counts with no personal identifiers, so there are no privacy risks. Key limitations include incomplete global coverage, variation in duration‑of‑marriage categories, and differences in national divorce laws and registration practices.

Comprehensive metadata and data‑transformation details are documented in the README.txt and data‑dictionary.csv, while full citations appear in the Blog Post tab.

Why explore the Seven-Year Itch?

The Seven‑Year Itch is one of those ideas that refuses to die — a cultural belief that marriages tend to break down around the seven‑year mark. It shows up in films, advice columns, and casual conversations, yet rarely gets tested with real data. That gap between myth and measurable reality makes it worth examining.

Understanding when marriages tend to end isn’t just trivia. Divorce timing reflects social norms, economic pressures, and how relationships evolve over the life course. If certain durations consistently show higher divorce counts, those patterns can reveal something meaningful about the stresses couples face at different stages.

Dataset for analysing the duration of marriage before divorce in Europe

The United Nations Statistics Division’s “Divorces by duration of marriage” dataset provides a rare opportunity to examine how long marriages last before ending across countries and over time. However, reporting completeness varies widely around the world. Many regions have sparse or inconsistent data, while Europe stands out as the area with the most comprehensive and continuous reporting of divorce‑duration statistics. To keep the analysis focused on contemporary patterns, this blog uses data from the most recent thirty years (1995–2025). For this reason, the analysis in this blog focuses on European countries, where the data are sufficiently complete to meaningfully explore whether a seven‑year peak in divorce timing actually appears — or whether the cultural myth collapses under empirical evidence.

What does divorce timing look like across Europe?

The bar graph below shows how common divorce is at different stages of marriage across Europe. The colours follow a light‑to‑dark gradient, with lighter shades representing earlier durations and darker shades representing longer ones.

Across Europe, divorces are least common in the very early years of marriage, with only a small share occurring within the first one or two years. The proportion grows gradually as marriages last longer, with noticeably more divorces occurring after several years together. Mid‑duration marriages show a moderate rise, but the largest share of divorces happens among couples who have been married for a decade or more. Overall, the pattern suggests that divorce becomes more likely as marriages accumulate time and complexity, rather than peaking sharply in the early or middle years.

Where in Europe do divorces occur earlier or later in a marriage?

While the Europe‑wide averages show that divorces tend to cluster in the long‑marriage years, this pattern is not necessarily identical across all countries. To understand these differences, it helps to look at the geography of divorce timing rather than just the overall distribution.

The map below shows the typical length of marriage before divorce in each European country, based on a weighted average of divorce‑duration categories from 1995 to 2025. To summarise the open‑ended “10+ years” category, I assume a midpoint of 15 years, reflecting the fact that many divorces in this group occur well beyond the ten‑year mark. Countries are coloured according to the resulting average number of years couples were married before divorcing, grouped into broad duration categories for clarity.

The map reveals a clear continental pattern in how long marriages typically last before divorce. Although every country experiences divorces at a range of durations, their weighted averages show that most European countries tend to divorce later, not earlier. A large share of countries fall into the 10+‑year category once the long‑marriage bucket is weighted appropriately, indicating that divorces commonly occur well after a decade of marriage. Others cluster in the 5–9‑year range, reflecting a mid‑marriage concentration.

This geographic pattern aligns with the broader trend visible in the earlier bar chart: Europe’s divorce timing is shaped primarily by mid‑ and long‑marriage breakdowns, with early‑marriage divorces playing a comparatively small role. While there is meaningful variation across countries, the overall picture is consistent—divorces across Europe tend to occur after many years of marriage, and long‑marriage divorces are far more widespread than early ones.

Has divorce timing shifted over the last 30 Years?

Now that we’ve seen the overall European pattern and the geographic differences between countries, the next question is whether divorce timing has changed over the last three decades. Social norms, economic conditions, and family structures have all evolved since the mid‑1990s, and these shifts may influence when marriages are most likely to end. To explore this, we track how the share of divorces in each duration category has changed from 1995 to 2025.

A clear pattern emerges from the time‑trend data. Early‑marriage divorces (0–4 years) remain consistently low throughout the entire period, with no indication that they are becoming more common. Mid‑marriage divorces in the 5–9‑year range form a stable middle layer, but the most striking feature of the chart is the persistent dominance of long‑marriage divorces. The 10+ category accounts for the largest share of divorces across all years.

So does the Seven-Year Itch exist?

Taken together, the evidence shows that the “Seven‑Year Itch” is more cultural shorthand than statistical reality. Across Europe, divorces do not cluster around the seven‑year mark; instead, the typical divorce occurs well beyond it. Both the geographic patterns and the long‑term trends point to the same conclusion: European marriages tend to last many years before breaking down, with long‑marriage divorces forming the largest share across almost all countries. Mid‑marriage divorces (5–9 years) remain more common than early ones, but there is no sharp spike at seven years—only a steady progression from early to mid‑marriage durations and a long, stable dominance of the 10+‑year category. In this sense, the Seven‑Year Itch captures a general idea about relationships changing over time, but it does not correspond to any distinct moment in the data.

Unsexy Parts of the Workflow (Question 5)

A lot of the work behind this project was slow and unglamorous. One of the most tedious tasks was dealing with the completely unstandardised duration‑of‑marriage categories: countries used overlapping ranges, inconsistent labels, and sometimes open‑ended buckets, which meant I had to manually inspect and recode many variations before they could be grouped into five consistent bins. Early on, my cleaning steps even produced non‑unique country–year–duration rows, so I spent a lot of time re‑running the pipeline, checking intermediate tables, and making sure each country–year ended up with exactly one row per bin. I also had to harmonise country names, verify that joins didn’t silently drop rows, and repeatedly confirm that percentages summed correctly. None of this was technically difficult, but it was repetitive, manual, and essential for producing a dataset I could trust.

Challenges Faced During the Analysis (Question 6)

The biggest challenge was realising that my initial cleaning pipeline was structured incorrectly. I originally computed percentages before standardising duration categories, which produced duplicated rows and totals that didn’t add up. Fixing this required rethinking the entire workflow and moving the standardisation step earlier so that each country–year–duration combination was unique before aggregation. Another challenge was dealing with incomplete reporting across countries, which forced me to narrow the scope to Europe and make explicit assumptions—such as assigning a midpoint of 15 years to the open‑ended “10+” category. These choices forced me to make several non‑trivial assumptions and analytical decisions—especially around how to treat open‑ended categories and incomplete reporting—and working through them was essential for producing results that were both defensible and meaningful.

Imperfect Parts and Future Improvements (Question 7)

A key limitation of my analysis is that it ultimately focuses only on Europe, because many countries outside the region reported incomplete or inconsistent duration breakdowns, making global comparisons unreliable within the scope of this assignment. Another imperfect aspect is the way I had to group duration‑of‑marriage categories: the raw data uses overlapping ranges, inconsistent labels, and open‑ended bins, so forcing them into five standard categories required simplifying assumptions that inevitably blur some nuance. Although the cleaning pipeline now ensures unique country–year–duration rows, the underlying binning still reflects compromises rather than perfectly aligned definitions. In future work, I would like to expand the analysis beyond Europe by developing methods to handle missing or partial duration distributions, and refine the duration modelling by estimating continuous distributions or using probabilistic approaches for partially overlapping bins instead of relying on fixed midpoints and manual recoding.

Commit #ae36bf2 - “change calculation method from peak duration to weighted average for more meaningful map”

The previous map I created from the incorrectly processed data showed almost every country falling into the “10+” category, which made the visualisation uninformative and suggested that my earlier method was masking variation.
